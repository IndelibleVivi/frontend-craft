# Design direction

Use for a new surface, an open design decision, or a substantial redesign. A
clear existing-system change does not need a new direction exercise.

## Find what makes this work succeed

Extract a compact working brief from available evidence: the deliverable and
viewing/usage context; audience and main task or experience; actual content;
accepted constraints and qualities; the important unresolved choice. Keep it
inline unless a maintained project record needs the result.

Distinguish a workbench, a reader, a public introduction, and a visual work.
They may share a brand while requiring different attention and interaction.
Ask which part would make the result fail even if everything around it were
finished. Examples: recognizing the active editing object; reading a dense
comparison; believing the optical depth of a procedural specimen. Render or
exercise that part before expanding decisions that depend on it. Complete the
full requested deliverable afterward.

If intent is missing, ask only what materially changes the result: sustained
use or brief viewing; intended audience; an unacceptable direction; which
quality of a reference matters. Do not force a questionnaire. A user who has
no preference can delegate a reversible choice; state the assumption, make a
judgeable slice, and keep provisional choices provisional.

## Compose from content

Use real or explicitly authorized representative content early: long names,
mixed scripts, uneven data, typical image ratios, actual state labels. Do not
make a flattering layout depend on short synthetic headings or missing content.

Translate intent into concrete relationships, not a list of adjectives:

| Decision | What to determine in this work | Useful rendered observation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Attention | What is seen first, understood second, acted on next? | Does the dominant region carry the subject/task, or compete with it? |
| Composition | What anchors the frame; what aligns, groups, contrasts, or intentionally breaks the grid? | Do region proportions and negative space make the subject legible? |
| Density | What must be compared simultaneously; what can wait? | Can users scan the actual workload without wandering or opening needless layers? |
| Typography | Which roles distinguish title, body, data, annotation, and controls? | Check line length, wrapping, rhythm, numerals, and mixed-script balance at reading size. |
| Color and material | What conveys identity, grouping, state, depth, or texture? | Does emphasis survive without color alone; does material read in the actual renderer? |
| Interaction | What object is affected, how is scope shown, and where is the result? | Can the action be discovered and its result/recovery understood? |
| Time | What is present immediately; how do entry, response, rest, and repetition feel? | Does motion clarify or express the work without obscuring action or requiring waiting? |

Choose a small coherent grammar: spacing relationships, type roles, alignment,
surface hierarchy, and a distinctive idea justified by the subject. Use the
project's existing tokens as the numeric authority. Do not establish a second
token table in prose.

Visual character can come from proportion, editorial order, cropping,
juxtaposition, a distinctive control, or temporal rhythm. Adding effects is
only one possible move. Restraint and richness both need intentional structure;
removing all ornament is not a universal route to quality. Explicitly requested
cards or gradients should be designed well, not resisted as an aesthetic sin.

## Explore only consequential uncertainty

Compare a few materially different approaches when the comparison can change
an expensive decision. Vary layout, task order, density, or interaction model,
not just accent colors. Use the same meaningful content for each. Most routine
tasks need one well-reasoned approach, not three options.

Make a consequential comparison perceptible at the actual viewing size. If the
variants collapse into the same impression, revise the comparison before
treating a selection as evidence of preference. Small but meaningful detail
changes are valid; imperceptible alternatives do not test different directions.

Do the implementation and rendering comparisons the agent can resolve itself.
Bring a concrete choice to the user only for a remaining taste/value decision,
with a recommendation and an explanation of what changes. Do not offload ten
unfinished alternatives and call that a successful first draft.

On strict reference work, measure the accepted composition rather than
inventing alternatives. On inspiration-led work, name the borrowed relationship
(for example, text/image tension), without importing unrelated branding,
ornament, or behavior. A supplied image does not settle its authority by itself.

## Make toward an explicit visual goal

Use this loop when the task aims at a particular aesthetic, expressive effect,
or reference language. Keep that goal visible throughout implementation, not
only in the initial brief. Distinguish exact replication from translating a
visual language to different content and from borrowing one isolated quality.
Confirm the scope from the request and supplied evidence; don't silently switch
between those modes when a result becomes difficult.

Use the actual reference evidence rather than a remembered style label. Reuse
an existing authorized reference collection for repeated work; record source,
capture surface, crop, viewport, and relevant state where they change the
interpretation. Preserve local reference files only when authorized and allowed
by the source/tool contract. An incomplete embedded canvas capture is not a
complete design, and its presentation frame is not automatically the target
artifact's frame.

1. **Describe the target in observable relationships.** From the actual
   reference, identify the relevant hierarchy, relative masses, typography,
   image treatment, negative space, depth, or movement. State what should carry
   into this work and what belongs only to the source context. A style name,
   accent color, or inventory of motifs is not enough to make this judgment.
2. **Choose a revealing input.** Use content that exposes the essential
   relationship at the intended viewing size. For a general-purpose creative
   tool, add a contrasting input once the direction works so the implementation
   doesn't depend on one forgiving subject.
3. **Render and identify the present gap.** Compare reference and current work
   under appropriate viewing conditions. Point to the relationship that still
   fails and explain its perceptual effect. “More blue” or “more dramatic” is a
   hypothesis to inspect, not proof of progress.
4. **Change the mechanism behind that gap.** Adjust composition, rendering,
   timing, or interaction where the cause lives. If the product lacks the
   required capability, develop it in the canonical path; see
   [visual works](visual-works.md) for returning experiments to usable controls.
5. **Recheck the goal and preserved qualities.** Observe the new render, the
   intended behavior, and any accepted qualities exposed to regression. Keep
   unresolved visual distance explicit even if technical checks pass. Stop at
   the requested outcome; agent judgment remains provisional where owner
   aesthetic acceptance has not been given.

Take visual grammar from the reference without importing its unrelated task
structure. A photo tool borrowing game-interface art direction still needs to
serve photo creation; a reference's menus, labels, and ornamental marks do not
automatically belong in every output. The workspace can keep its own identity.

Preserve intentional variation inside the visual grammar. Per-object color,
opacity, edge, or emphasis may carry identity and hierarchy; a global palette
replacement or uniform inactive opacity can erase those differences. Establish
which properties the theme owns and which authored exceptions should survive,
then inspect both selected and unselected states in the real render.

If iterations repeatedly converge on a familiar generic layout, return to the
goal and the actual reference before adding more settings or motifs. Likewise,
“more negative space” calls for purposeful spatial tension and hierarchy, not
an arbitrary emptier frame. Preserve the original product purpose while
changing the mechanisms needed to reach it.

## Make the first presentation earn its place

Before showing it, inspect the decisive quality in the render, with actual
content and the intended viewing scale. Check the dominant hierarchy and the
first meaningful action. Resolve obvious in-scope visual and functional defects
internally; a first presentation may follow several internal iterations.

Check execution, not a tally of stylistic ingredients. Large type can have
weak rhythm; a gradient can flatten a composition; decorative labels can compete
with the actual subject. Inspect proportions, grouping, texture and active
control states in context. Describe the quality as a supported agent judgment
until the owner evaluates it; do not turn a technically successful sample into
a taste reference for future work without that evidence.

Keep iteration economical: identify the current defect, make a coherent change,
observe it, then stop if the brief is satisfied. Label any remaining limitation.
Do not hide incomplete work behind a conservative design, a proposal label, a
large settings panel, or reduced scope. When a prototype was the requested
deliverable, state what it demonstrates and what is not integrated.
